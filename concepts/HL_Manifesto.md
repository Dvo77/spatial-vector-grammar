The HL System Manifesto
by Daniel DeVoy


If we don't use it, we'll lose it.


My great grandfather was a doctor named Piper.

Country vet. Sharon, Wisconsin. He's gone now.

But his screws aren't.

They're in a drawer in my shop — a beat-up metal cabinet, army green, labeled OLG 1. Old Granddad. Dr. Piper. Wood screws, carriage bolts, cabinet knobs. A lifetime of building things, sorted by hand, stored with intention.

I don't know which screws he touched. I don't know what he built with them. Nobody wrote it down.

But I kept them. And I named the cabinet after him. Because throwing them out felt like losing something I couldn't name — and I'd already lost enough.



His daughter — my grandma Chaibonneau — her stuff has its own kit now. A logo. An ID number. FC-CHAR-001. Every item that belonged to her travels with her name attached. The vessel doesn't matter. The record does.

I didn't invent this to be sentimental.

I invented it because I watched things disappear and I got tired of it.


The Tractor
Dr. Piper bought a 1939 Ford 9N tractor sometime in the early 1940s.

He wrote a letter about it — to a family member, by hand. I have that letter.

In it, he explains that he had to spend $700 he barely had. The Depression had not fully loosened its grip. The taxes on the land were costing more than the land could produce. The horses couldn't keep up anymore. The economy was getting that bad.

Seven hundred dollars. For a tractor. To survive.

He wrote it down. He kept the paperwork. And somehow — across decades, across a farm sale, across everything that gets lost between a man's life and his great-grandchildren — it survived.

When the family farm in Sharon was sold, I recovered that tractor. It wasn't running. I brought it home. I restored it to original condition. I used it the way it needed to be used.

I still have it. I still use it. It has its own page in my personal Wiki. Dr. Piper has a legacy page. The letter is documented.

That tractor is 85 years old. It runs. It has a record. It has a name.

That is what the HL System is trying to make possible for everything — not just tractors owned by people stubborn enough to go find them.


The Problem
There are sixty tape measures in my shop. Probably more. Some are antique. Some I don't even know I have yet — an AI running image recognition would find them before I would.

I built modular benches I call MaxFlow — wood frames with tall backs, each one its own kit, lockable into a single long bench or broken apart for specific tasks. I built wall sorts. Bin systems. I named every one of them because they earned names.

And none of it — none of it — was recorded in a way that would survive me.

If I disappeared tomorrow, someone would walk into that shop and see a pile of tools. They wouldn't see a system. They wouldn't see the intention. They wouldn't know what OLG 1 meant, or why the Chaibonneau kit has a logo, or why the screw drawer is labeled the way it is.

They'd see stuff. And they'd start making decisions. And things would get thrown out.

That's not grief. That's just entropy. Untruth allowed to persist.


The Solution Isn't Software
I didn't build an app.

I built a grammar.

A simple, free, open-source way to give anything — any object, any space, any drawer full of old screws — four things:

A location (where it lives)
A name (what it is, noun first, always)
A relationship (what it belongs to, what belongs to it)
A record (that it existed, and why it mattered)

That's it. That's the whole system.

GR-WS-S2-B0

Garage → Workshop zone → South wall, column 2 → Second shelf → Surface

FASTENER-SCREW-WOOD-MIXED-VINTAGE — OLG1

VEHICLE-TRACTOR-FORD-9N-1939 — DR-PIPER

Human readable. Machine readable. AI readable. Works on paper. Works in a phone note. Works in a GitHub repo. Works in a 13-tier personal Wiki. Works on a homelab running Llama on a 3080.

The iron rule: No rule can make the system more complicated than no system at all.


What This Is Really About
It's not about tools.

It's about the fact that we are surrounded by the evidence of lives lived — and we keep losing it by accident.

It's about the $700 letter in a box that almost didn't survive. About a tractor that almost got scrapped. About a country vet's carriage bolts sitting in a drawer with no name on them — until someone finally put one there.

It's about the holiday decorations in unlabeled boxes that get sorted wrong every year because nobody left instructions.

It's about the factory your grandfather built, the layout he designed, the logic he put into every wall and bench and zone — gone when the building came down because no one mapped it.

It's about the knowledge that lives in a person and goes with them when they leave.

We don't have to let it go.


Asha
There's a word in Zoroastrian philosophy: Asha.

It means truth. Order. A self-correcting system that fights entropy not by brute force — but by staying honest.

The opposite is Druj. Corruption. Untruth allowed to persist. The unlabeled box. The forgotten name. The drawer that says "misc" forever.

I'm not religious. But this framework fit too well to ignore.

The HL System is an Asha system. It names things. It records them. It makes the truth about a space or an object or a person's life permanent — not because we forced it, but because we built a structure that makes honesty easier than forgetting.

Every item named is a small act of truth maintenance.

Every address assigned is a small act of preservation.

Every letter scanned and linked to a tractor's record is proof that the system works — and that it always could have.


Who This Is For
This is for the person standing in their father's garage after the funeral, not knowing where anything is.

This is for the builder who's tired of reinventing their own shop every five years.

This is for the family that wants grandma's holiday kit to stay grandma's holiday kit — with her name on it, her logic intact, her choices honored.

This is for the kid who will someday inherit a drawer full of screws and deserve to know whose they were.

This is for anyone who ever looked at a pile of stuff and knew — knew — that there was a system in there, if someone had just written it down.

And it's for the person who finds something at an estate sale with a name on it — and can look that name up, and find the family, and return it. Or at least let them see it.


How to Start
You don't need software.

You don't need to label everything today.

You don't need a homelab or a Wiki or sixty tape measures.

You just need one space. One zone. One shelf. One item with a name and a home.

Start there.

Download Prompt 1. Paste it into any AI. Push go.

The grammar will do the rest.
